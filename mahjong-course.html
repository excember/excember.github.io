First suit: the Dots, sometimes called Circles. Each Dot tile shows its number as a pattern of circles — one circle for the one, two for the two, all the way up to a three-by-three grid for the nine. And there's a shortcut printed right on the tile: a small number in the corner. Trust the corner. One thing to watch: the one Dot is a single big medallion, and brand-new players sometimes mistake it for a flower. If it's one large circle, it's the one Dot. Four copies of every number — thirty-six Dots in all.

Suit 2 of 3 · Bams (Bamboo)

2–9 show that many bamboo sticks; the corner number confirms. Four of each — 36 Bams total.

★ THE ONE EVERYONE MISSES: the 1 Bam is a bird. There are no bamboo sticks on it — it’s a bird on a perch. New players discard it thinking it’s a bonus tile, or fail to call it. Drill it until the bird instantly reads as “1 Bam.”

Second suit: the Bams, short for bamboo. Twos through nines show that many bamboo sticks — count the sticks or read the corner number. But here's the one everybody misses, so lean in: the one Bam is a bird. No sticks at all — a bird sitting on a perch. New players throw it away thinking it's a decoration, or stare right past it when they need it. Burn this in now: bird equals one Bam. Four of each, thirty-six total.

Top character = the number (一 through 九). The bottom red character 萬 (“wan,” ten-thousand) marks the suit. You never need to read Chinese: the Arabic numeral in the corner tells you the number.

Four of each — 36 Craks total. If the tile has the red 萬 at the bottom, it’s a Crak; say the corner number and “Crak.”

Third suit: the Craks, short for characters. Each Crak has two Chinese characters: the top one is the number, and the bottom one — the red character, wan — is the suit marker. MODULE 3

The card, sets & jokers

You win by matching the card
Every legal hand is printed on the players’ card (most tables use the NMJL card, reissued every year). Your 14 tiles must match one listed hand exactly — no freestyle combinations. Hands are worth 25–75 points, printed beside each hand.

The building blocks

Pair (2) · identical
Pung (3)
Kong (4)
Quint (5) · only possible with jokers
Exposed vs concealed: call a discard to complete a pung/kong/quint → that set is exposed face-up on your rack. Hands marked C (concealed) on the card allow no exposures — you may only claim a discard for mahjong itself. Exposed hands are easier; concealed hands score more. A “sextet” (6) appears on some cards — same idea, jokers required. Your table’s card governs.
▸
INSTRUCTOR · AUDIO TRANSCRIPT

So what are you actually building? Here's the heart of American mahjong: you win by matching your fourteen tiles, exactly, to one of the hands printed on the card — most tables use the National Mah Jongg League card, which is reissued every year. No freestyle. Your building blocks are simple: a pair is two identical tiles, a pung is three, a kong is four, and a quint is five — and since only four copies of any tile exist, a quint is only possible with jokers. One more distinction that shapes your whole game: exposed versus concealed. When you call a discard to finish a pung, kong, or quint, that set gets flipped face-up on your rack — exposed. Hands marked C on the card are concealed: no exposures allowed, and you can only claim a discard for the win itself. Concealed is harder, and it pays more.

Joker rules — strict, and worth memorizing

✓ LEGAL
Kong of 7 Bams using a joker — jokers fill any spot in a group of 3 or more (pung, kong, quint, sextet).
✗ NEVER
No joker in a pair. No joker as a single (including year, NEWS, or 2026-style number hands). This is the #1 joker error.
The joker exchange — free upgrades
On your turn (after drawing), if any player’s exposed set contains a joker and you hold the matching natural tile, you may swap your tile for that joker — including from your own exposures.
A discarded joker is just a dead discard: no one may call it, and it stops being wild.
Jokers are never passed in the Charleston — receive one by mistake and it goes straight back.
▸
INSTRUCTOR · AUDIO TRANSCRIPT

Joker rules — strict, short, and worth memorizing, because joker mistakes are the most common fouls in the game. Rule one: jokers live only in groups of three or more. A pung, a kong, a quint — perfect. A pair? Never. A single tile? Never. Rule two — and this one wins games — the joker exchange. On your turn, after you draw, if any exposed set on the table shows a joker and you're holding the matching real tile, you can swap your tile for that joker. Even from your own exposures. A free wild tile, just for paying attention. Rule three: a discarded joker is dead — nobody can call it, and its wildness is gone. And rule four: jokers never travel in the Charleston. If someone passes you one by mistake, straight back it goes.

▸
INSTRUCTOR · AUDIO TRANSCRIPT

Now the famous part: the Charleston, the pre-game tile swap. First Charleston — this one's required: pass three tiles to your right, then three across the table, then three to your left. Right, across, left. Second Charleston — optional, and here's the key: it takes everyone. Any single player can call it off. If it runs, it mirrors back: left, across, right. Two special moves. The blind pass: on the last pass of either Charleston, if you love your hand, you may pass along tiles you just received without even looking at them. And the courtesy pass: after all the passing ends, you and the player across from you may swap zero to three tiles — and the lower number rules. You want three, they want one? You each pass one. And the golden rule one more time: jokers never pass.

Let's play. On your turn: draw one tile from the wall, rack it, and discard one face-up in the middle — naming it out loud. "Six Dot." "Soap." Then it's the next player's turn, moving counter-clockwise. Simple rhythm, one golden number: after your discard you hold exactly thirteen. Now, calling. Only the most recent discard is ever up for grabs — the moment the next player draws, it's gone. You can call a discard to expose a pung, kong, or quint, or to declare mahjong. A pair? Only if it wins you the game on the spot. If two players want the same tile, mahjong beats an exposure, and otherwise it goes to whoever's turn comes next. And the thing to fear: the dead hand. Wrong tile count — twelve tiles, fifteen tiles — and you're out: you stop playing, keep your tiles hidden, and you still pay the winner. The cure costs one second: count before every discard.
MODULE 6

Winning & scoring

Declare it
14th tile completes a card hand → say “Mah Jongg” and show it. The table verifies against the card. Its printed value (25–75) is the base.
Win by discard
Discarder pays 2×Other two pay 1×
Throwing the winning tile is expensive — defense matters.
Win by self-pick
Everyone pays 2×
Draw your own winning tile from the wall and all three opponents pay double.
Bonus & edge cases
Jokerless: winning with zero jokers doubles the value again — bonuses stack. (Doesn’t apply to Singles-and-Pairs hands, which can’t use jokers anyway.)
Wall game: tiles run out with no winner → nobody pays; shuffle and redeal.
Values and hands come from the card in play — confirm the year before starting.
WORKED EXAMPLE
25-pt hand, won on a discard: discarder pays 50 · others pay 25 each = 100 in.
Same hand self-picked & jokerless: 25 × 2 × 2 = 100 from each player.
▸
INSTRUCTOR · AUDIO TRANSCRIPT

The good part: getting paid. When your fourteenth tile completes a card hand, say "Mah Jongg," show your tiles, and the table checks them against the card. The printed value — twenty-five to seventy-five points — is your base. Then how you won sets the multiplier. Won on somebody's discard? That player pays you double, and the other two pay single — which is why careless discards are expensive. Drew the winning tile yourself off the wall? Self-pick: everybody pays double. And if your winning hand used zero jokers, double it again — the bonuses stack. Quick example: a twenty-five point hand, self-picked, jokerless — twenty-five, doubled, doubled — that's a hundred from every player. One more outcome to know: if the wall runs out with no winner, that's a wall game. Nobody pays. Shuffle up, rotate East, go again.

Playing with strangers

Ask before the first Charleston

1

“Are we on this year’s NMJL card?” Hands and values change every year — bring or borrow the right one.
2
House rules?
Courtesy pass in use? Any local joker or payment quirks? Thirty seconds now prevents every argument later.
3
Stakes?
Chips, coins, or just score? Confirm what a point is worth before anyone owes anything.
Table etiquette
Keep pace — draw, decide, discard; the table has a rhythm.
Name every discard clearly; call claims promptly.
Racks hide your hand; comment on no one’s tiles but your own.
Win and lose the same way: pleasantly.
THE ONE-SECOND SELF-CHECK
Before every discard: Do I have 13? Is this the tile I mean to throw? That glance is the whole difference between a smooth player and a dead hand.
